1 · How a typical shodan test runs

Formats differ, but most Shito-ryu dan gradings follow this flow. Published syllabi (e.g., Shitoryu Karate Canada, which is WSKF/JKF-Shitokai affiliated, and Karate Canada's Shito-ryu dan guideline) list kihon, kata, bunkai, kumite and knowledge (history/terminology) as the core components.

Typical order

  1. Rei & line-up: bow in, kneel (seiza), mokuso, bow to shomen/sensei/panel.
  2. Kihon: stationary basics, then ido kihon (moving) and renzoku (combinations) up and down the floor, on count and without.
  3. Kata: usually one or two required (shitei) kata plus one of your choice (tokui) and/or one picked by the examiner. Panels often call a random Pinan.
  4. Bunkai: applications of a required kata (often Bassai Dai) and your tokui kata with a partner.
  5. Kumite: prearranged (ippon/sanbon, sometimes Pinan hokei kumite) then jiyu kumite (free sparring), sometimes several rounds or partners.
  6. Knowledge: oral questions (history, terminology, principles); some federations add a short written essay.
  7. Closing: bow out; results may come the same day or later.

What examiners look for

  • Stances: correct depth, width, weight distribution; stable transitions.
  • Technique: correct path, chamber and hikite, target level.
  • Kime: focused power at impact, then relax.
  • Hips & timing: body moves as one; hand and foot finish together.
  • Kata accuracy: sequence, embusen (start = finish point), rhythm, speed contrast, breathing.
  • Eyes & spirit: chakugan (look before turning), kiai, zanshin.
  • Understanding: bunkai that works at realistic distance.
  • Kumite: distance (maai), timing, control, sportsmanship.
  • Attitude: etiquette, effort when tired, humility.
WKF kata judging uses a similar split: technical performance (stances, techniques, transitions, timing, breathing, kime, conformance to style) and athletic performance (strength, speed, balance).
Example published standards (reference only): Shitoryu Karate Canada lists shodan kata as mandatory Bassai Dai and Seienchin plus a tokui kata from Pinan 1–5, Juroku, Jitte, Itosu Rohai Shodan, Naihanchin Shodan, Matsumura Rohai or Shinsei; Bassai Dai bunkai; Pinan hokei kumite; point sparring. Karate Canada's Shito-ryu guideline also lists Seienchin and Bassai for shodan, plus bunkai and jiyu kumite. Other groups (e.g., Shito-ryu Genbu-kai) let the examiner pick from ~15 kata including Jion, Wanshu, Wankan and Kushanku (Kosokun) Dai. Jion and Matsukaze are nidan-level requirements in some syllabi.

Names follow common Shito-ryu usage; dojos differ. Shito-ryu often calls the outward middle block yoko uke or soto uke and the inward one uchi uke / yoko uchi uke. Use Sensei's names.

Typical shodan combination drills (example from a published syllabus)

  • Moto-dachi chudan kamae: mae geri → yoko geri → mawashi geri on one leg without putting it down.
  • Zenkutsu dachi forward, back and diagonally back: gedan barai + gyaku-zuki, yoko uke + gyaku-zuki, uchi uke + gyaku-zuki, age uke + gyaku-zuki.
  • Neko-ashi chudan yoko uke → front-leg mae geri → jodan mawashi geri → land in moto-dachi with gyaku-zuki → shift back and repeat.
Source: Shitoryu Karate Canada student manual (shodan). Katsu Dojo's combinations may differ.

4 · Bunkai prompts

Bunkai ("analysis") means demonstrating what kata movements do against a partner. Be ready to show at least the required kata (often Bassai Dai) and your tokui kata. Work these prompts with a partner at realistic distance and speed.

General prompts

  • What is the attack, and from which direction of the embusen?
  • What does the hikite do: grab, pull, trap?
  • Why this stance here: closing in, moving off-line, dropping weight?
  • Which of the five uke principles is shown?
  • What's the finish: strike, throw, lock or escape?
  • Can you show a second layer, like a "block" used as a strike or a grab release?

Kata-specific prompts

  • Pinan Nidan: first gedan barai + tettsui against a punch; each turn = a threat from a new direction.
  • Pinan Shodan/Godan: the shuto sequence; juji uke against a front kick; age-uke with hikite pulling the attacker in.
  • Bassai Dai: the opening drive as closing distance; switching uchi/soto uke with hip reversal; sukui uke against a kick; two-hand movements as locks or throws.
  • Seienchin: escapes from wrist grabs in the slow shiko-dachi sections; low pulls as off-balancing; close elbows and hammer-fists.
  • Jion: kakiwake uke against a two-hand grab or push; manji uke against attacks from two sides.
  • Matsukaze: side-to-side punches and leg-switching kicks as continuous pressure.
  • Naihanchin: fighting with your back to a wall; nami-gaeshi as a kick check.
Presentation tips: bow to your partner, say what you're showing ("Bassai Dai, opening sequence"), demonstrate slowly once, then at speed with control, and finish with zanshin. Agree on the attacks with your partner beforehand. Simple and convincing beats flashy.

5 · Kumite (sparring)

Ippon kumite

One-step prearranged. The attacker announces the target and steps in with one technique (e.g., jodan oi-zuki); the defender blocks and counters once with kime, then zanshin. Graders watch distance, timing, a decisive counter and eye contact.

Sanbon kumite

Three-step. The attacker advances with three attacks; the defender retreats with three blocks and counters on the last. Keep stances deep and lines straight; the final counter must be sharp.

Jiyu ippon / jiyu kumite

Jiyu ippon: free movement with one announced attack. Jiyu kumite: free sparring, which most Shito-ryu federations expect at shodan. Some syllabi also include hokei kumite (prearranged Pinan applications).

Tuesday sparring tips

  • Treat Tuesdays as a test rehearsal. Ask a senior to call hajime/yame and watch one thing you're fixing.
  • Control first. Examiners penalise excessive contact far more than missed points. Skin-touch to jodan, controlled to chudan.
  • Own the distance (maai). Bounce in moto-dachi just out of range; enter as they move.
  • Three go-to combos: kizami-zuki → gyaku-zuki; mae-ashi geri → gyaku-zuki; jodan feint → chudan mawashi geri.
  • One counter you own: drill a single block-counter (e.g., nagashi uke + gyaku-zuki) until it's automatic.
  • Finish every exchange with your guard up and zanshin. Don't turn away or drop your hands.
  • Conditioning: spar 3–4 × 2-minute rounds so you aren't gassed on test day.
  • Gear & safety: mouthguard and the mitts/foot protection your dojo requires; hydrate in the Gainesville heat.
  • Etiquette: bow in and out, acknowledge good techniques, accept the referee's call.

Know the calls: hajime (begin), yame (stop), tsuzukete (continue), aka/ao (red/blue). WKF scoring: yuko 1 pt, waza-ari 2 pts, ippon 3 pts.

6 · Kobudo (weapons) note

Katsu Dojo offers Okinawan kobudo: bo (6-ft staff), tonfa, sai, kama, nunchaku and tanbo (short stick). Daniel trains weapons after Wednesday class. Many karate shodan tests do not require kobudo, but some dojos ask for a basic weapon kata or demonstration, so ask Sensei whether it's on the test.

  • Bow to the weapon before and after use; never step over a weapon; carry it under control.
  • Weapon work should show the same empty-hand fundamentals: stances, hips, kime.
  • Know the names of your weapon kata and a line of history if asked. Common Okinawan examples include Shushi no Kon (bo) and Tsuken Shitahaku no Sai (sai); Katsu Dojo's own list may differ.
  • Use Wednesdays to rehearse one weapon form calmly. It's good grip and forearm conditioning too.

Dojo etiquette (reigi)

  • Bow (ritsurei) when entering or leaving the dojo and stepping on or off the mat.
  • Be on time. If you're late, kneel at the edge and wait for Sensei's permission to join.
  • Clean gi, trimmed nails, no jewelry, belt tied properly.
  • Line up by rank following your dojo's custom.
  • Answer instructions with "Osu" or "Hai, Sensei," whichever your dojo uses.
  • Bow to partners before and after drills. Never leave the mat without permission.
  • Turn away from the front to fix your gi or belt.
  • Respect weapons and equipment; help clean the dojo.

Dojo kun (common version)

Many karate dojos, including many Shito-ryu ones, recite a five-line dojo kun. Wording varies. A widely used form:

  1. Hitotsu, jinkaku kansei ni tsutomuru koto: Seek perfection of character.
  2. Hitotsu, makoto no michi o mamoru koto: Be faithful; follow the way of truth.
  3. Hitotsu, doryoku no seishin o yashinau koto: Cultivate a spirit of effort.
  4. Hitotsu, reigi o omonzuru koto: Respect others; value etiquette.
  5. Hitotsu, kekki no yu o imashimuru koto: Refrain from violent behavior.
Confirm Katsu Dojo's own dojo kun (if it has one) and memorise that version.

8 · History of Shito-ryu

  • Founder: Kenwa Mabuni (摩文仁賢和), born in Shuri, Okinawa, 14 Nov 1889; died 23 May 1952. (Most sources; one federation site says 1893.)
  • Teacher 1: Anko Itosu (糸洲安恒, c. 1831–1915), Shuri-te. Mabuni began training with him around age 13. Itosu brought karate into Okinawan schools in the early 1900s and created the Pinan kata. Itosu-kei kata: Pinan, Naihanchin, Bassai, Kosokun, Jion, Jitte, Rohai, Chinto…
  • Teacher 2: Kanryo Higaonna (東恩納寛量, 1853–1915), Naha-te. Mabuni began with him around age 20 (c. 1909), introduced by his friend Chojun Miyagi, later the founder of Goju-ryu. Higaonna-kei kata: Sanchin, Seienchin, Seipai, Kururunfa, Suparinpei…
  • Other influences: Mabuni also learned from other Okinawan teachers (e.g., Seisho Aragaki) and absorbed Chinese White Crane forms (e.g., Nipaipo, Papuren). That's why Shito-ryu has one of the largest kata syllabi, often quoted at 50+ kata.
  • To mainland Japan: he moved to Osaka around 1929 (some sources say 1928). His early name for the art was Hanko-ryu ("half-hard style"); the WSKF history says he first taught under the name Goju-ryu.
  • The name: in the 1930s the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai pushed styles to register names. Mabuni chose Shito (糸東) from the first kanji of Itosu (糸, also read "shi") and Higaonna (東, also read "to"). It honours both teachers rather than having a literal meaning. Ryu = school or style.
  • Dates you may hear: c. 1931 (Dai Nippon Karatedo Kai study group in Kansai); 1934 (name "Shito-ryu" adopted per WSKF, and the Yoshukan dojo opened in Osaka); 1939 (formally registered with the Butoku Kai, Mabuni awarded Renshi). Katsu Dojo's website says "founded by Kenwa Mabuni in 1928." Safe answer: "Founded by Kenwa Mabuni in Osaka in the late 1920s–1930s; named Shito-ryu in the 1930s and registered in 1939." If Sensei teaches a specific date, use that.
  • Characteristics: blends Shuri-te's linear power and long stances with Naha-te's circular, close-range, hard-soft and breathing methods; Mabuni's uke no go gensoku; strong emphasis on bunkai.
  • After Mabuni: his sons Kenei Mabuni and Kenzo Mabuni carried on the style. Major branches include Shito-kai (Manzo Iwata; today's JKF Shito-kai/WSKF), Hayashi-ha (Teruo Hayashi), Tani-ha/Shukokai (Chojiro Tani) and Itosu-kai (Ryusho Sakagami).
  • Today: Shito-ryu is one of the four major JKF/WKF styles, with Goju-ryu, Shotokan and Wado-ryu. Bassai Dai and Seienchin are its standard designated (shitei) kata.
